Papa v Commissioner of Police

15 Oct 2026Supreme Court of New South Wales[2026] NSWSC 1263Migration: court challengeAdministrative law
Before: Chen J Decision: (1) Order the further amended summons filed 18 September 2026 b
The plaintiff sought judicial review of decisions revoking his Category AB firearms licence, including the original revocation and an internal review affirming it. The Supreme Court held only the internal review decision was operative and reviewable, found no failure to consider mandatory considerations or legal unreasonableness, and dismissed the summons.
Key holding

Where an original administrative decision is affirmed on internal review under s 53 of the ADR Act 1997 (NSW), only the review decision is legally operative and amenable to judicial review.

That conclusion, which was essentially argued by the defendant, means that, in the present circumstances, the second is the operative decision. It is only that decision which is amenable to judicial review.

Tran and Australian Skills Quality Authority

12 Oct 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2300Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa nominationAdministrative law
Mr Tran sought review of ASQA’s decision to cancel his Certificate III in Carpentry, obtained via RPL from Kingsway, a deregistered RTO. Although the Tribunal found the s 56(1)(a) ground was established because Kingsway did not provide or arrange the necessary assessment, it exercised the discretion not to cancel in exceptional circumstances. The cancellation decision was set aside and the qualification is to be treated as a valid VET RPL qualification.
Key holding

Even when the s 56(1)(a) ground is established, the Tribunal may exercise the s 56(1) discretion not to cancel a VET qualification in exceptional circumstances, including clear lack of applicant fault, misleading RTO conduct, extensive genuine industry experience, and a public interest that does not require cancellation.

The Tribunal is satisfied that the discretion under subsection 56(1) of the NVR Act not to cancel the Qualification can be safely and appropriately exercised in the exceptional circumstances of this case. That discretion may be exercised consistent with the public interest, given that Mr Tran possesses the requisite training, skills and work experience to have been awarded the Qualification on an RPL basis.

Hovius v Speno Rail Maintenance Australia Pty Ltd

12 Oct 2026Supreme Court of New South Wales[2026] NSWSC 1152Personal injuryAdministrative law
Before: Harrison AJ Decision: See [90] Catchwords: ADMINISTRATIVE LAW – judicial review
Mr Hovius sought judicial review of a Personal Injury Commission Medical Appeal Panel decision assessing his permanent impairment at 18% after workplace knee/hip injuries and scarring. The Supreme Court found the Appeal Panel failed to assess impairment in accordance with the workers compensation guidelines, failed to address all appeal grounds including pain and hip, and failed to give adequate reasons. The Court held he was entitled to the relief sought, including an extension of time.
Key holding

The workers compensation guidelines have delegated legislative force, and an Appeal Panel’s failure to assess permanent impairment in accordance with them can amount to a constructive failure to exercise jurisdiction.

Those guidelines have the force or effect of delegated legislation and failure to carry out an assessment in accordance with them may constitute a constructive failure to exercise jurisdiction.

Williams v Companies Auditors Disciplinary Board

9 Oct 2026Federal Court of Australia (Full Court)[2026] FCAFC 136Migration: court challengeAdministrative law
The Full Court dismissed an appeal from the primary judge’s refusal of judicial review of the Companies Auditors Disciplinary Board’s decision to cancel Mr Williams’ registration as a company auditor. The Board found he failed adequately and properly to perform his duties as auditor under s 1292(1)(d)(i) of the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth), principally because he was Engagement Partner for the 2012 LM Audit. The appeal concerned whether that impermissibly imposed vicarious responsibility for work done by others.
Key holding

Under s 1292(1)(d)(i) of the Corporations Act, an audit Engagement Partner may be held responsible for the overall quality and sufficiency of the audit and audit opinion, irrespective of who performed particular tasks, as a leadership responsibility rather than vicarious liability.

The Engagement Partner's responsibility for the audit is not confined to work personally undertaken by the Engagement Partner. The intention is plain: to impose responsibility on the Engagement Partner for the audit overall irrespective of who conducted particular tasks in the course of the audit in recognition of the Engagement Partner's leadership role (at [50]-[53] of the primary Judge's Decision).

Darjilla Pty Ltd trading as Corowa Supa IGA v Annamneni [2026] NSWSC 402 (23 April 2026)

23 Apr 2026Supreme Court of New South Wales[2026] NSWSC 402Migration: court challengeAdministrative law
Before: Schmidt AJ
Darjilla Pty Ltd t/as Corowa Supa IGA sought judicial review of a NCAT decision granting Ravi Babu Annamneni a packaged liquor licence for the South Corowa General Store. IGA alleged NCAT failed to consider the mandatory prohibition in s 31(2) of the Liquor Act 2007 (NSW) on licences for service stations or take-away food shops. The Supreme Court found IGA had standing and granted an extension of time but dismissed the application, holding s 31(2) was not engaged and no jurisdictional error was established.
Key holding

Where a mandatory statutory prohibition is not engaged by the evidence and issues before the tribunal, its non-consideration does not establish jurisdictional error.

It follows that it cannot be concluded in these judicial review proceedings that the prohibition s 31(2) imposed, which was not raised by the material or the cases NCAT had to consider, established the claimed jurisdictional error.

Rivas v Minister for Finance [2026] FCA 388 (7 April 2026)

7 Apr 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 388Migration: court challengeAdministrative law
Ms Rivas sought judicial review under s 39B of the Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) of the Minister’s decision under ss 22(2) and 23 of the Extradition Act 1988 (Cth) to surrender her to Chile for alleged aggravated kidnapping. She argued the extradition offence should be characterised as a crime against humanity and that the Minister failed to consider a specialty risk under the Chile extradition Treaty. Lee J dismissed the application with costs.
Key holding

The extradition offence is the offence identified in the request and accompanying documents; a requesting State’s contextual classification of the conduct as a crime against humanity does not transform that offence or require the Minister to proceed on that basis.

The fact that the same conduct may, under the law of the requesting State, be regarded as occurring in circumstances which justify its classification as a crime against humanity does not transmute the extradition offence into a different offence, nor does it require the Minister to proceed upon that footing.
