Manpreet Kaur (Migration)

25 Sep 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2386Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewVisa integrityMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a refusal of a Subclass 500 (Student) visa for Ms Kaur, an Indian citizen who has lived in Australia as a student since 2018. Although the Tribunal accepted she was enrolled in a registered course, it was not satisfied she genuinely intended to stay temporarily because her latest lower-level course appeared designed to extend her residence rather than advance her studies. The Tribunal affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

Under cl 500.212, all of paras (a), (b) and (c) must be satisfied, so failure to establish cl 500.212(a) alone is sufficient to fail the genuine applicant criterion.

To satisfy cl 500.212, all of subcls (a), (b) and (c) must be met and an unfavourable finding in relation to either (a) or (b) forecloses the possibility of a favourable outcome.

Bekir Pty Ltd (Migration)

25 Aug 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2393Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa nominationVisa integrityMigration
In Bekir Pty Ltd (Migration) [2026] ARTA 2393, the sponsor sought review of a delegate’s decision to bar it for six months under s 140M of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth), based on an alleged failure to ensure its sponsored employee worked as a Chef contrary to reg 2.86 of the Migration Regulations 1994 (Cth). The Tribunal found Mr Singh’s duties substantially aligned with ANZSCO Chef and no reg 2.86 breach, while a Fair Work Ombudsman NES contravention under reg 2.92(2) did not warrant any s 140M action. The Tribunal set aside the bar.
Key holding

Whether an employee performs the nominated occupation of Chef is assessed against ANZSCO duties, skills, qualifications and experience, not an additional delegate-imposed requirement of dish complexity.

Therefore, having proper regard to the ANZSCO guidance, a Chef is a Chef having regard to their skills, qualifications, experience and the performance of the associated duties.

Cyber Computer Recycling & Disposal Pty. Ltd. Atf The Cyber System Family Trust (Migration)

6 Aug 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2373Tribunal reviewVisa integrityVisa cancellationVisa nominationMigration
The Tribunal reconsidered, on remittal from the Federal Circuit and Family Court, a delegate's decision to cancel Cyber Computer Recycling & Disposal Pty Ltd's approval as a standard business sponsor under s 140M of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth). The delegate and a differently constituted Tribunal had relied on alleged underpayment, "pay for visa" arrangements, a role change, unpaid overtime and non-disclosure of an associated entity's insolvency.
Key holding

Where the Minister has subsequently granted the sponsor a fresh sponsorship approval notwithstanding known adverse information, the Tribunal should not readily find that it is not reasonable to disregard that same information for the purposes of the sponsorship criteria.

While the Tribunal has found that there existed adverse information in that the applicant failed to declare the information relating to Jomol Shibu and ITS IT Australia in the manner required, it would be counterintuitive for the Tribunal to now make a decision that it was not reasonable to disregard that information in circumstances where the Department effectively has disregarded the same information in approving the applicant's most recent standard business sponsorship approval.

Nisha (Migration)

3 Jul 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2356Tribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewVisa integrityMigration
Mrs Batulan Nisha applied for a Partner (Temporary) (Class UK) visa (Subclass 820) in December 2018 on the basis of her marriage to Mr John Mitchell. A delegate refused the visa solely because there was no evidence the sponsor was an Australian citizen, permanent resident or eligible New Zealand citizen. On review the applicant produced movement records and citizenship evidence, and the Tribunal, being satisfied the marriage was valid and the relationship genuine and continuing, set aside the refusal and remitted the matter.
Key holding

The absence of joint major assets or liabilities does not tell against the genuineness of a spousal relationship where it is explained by the parties' age and limited income, the reg 1.15A(3) factors being weighed in light of their actual circumstances.

Given their age and limited income, the Tribunal considers the findings that they have no joint major assets or liabilities are not indicative that they are not in a genuine and continuing relationship.

2407106 (Migration)

1 Jul 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2270Tribunal reviewVisa integrityVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a Student (Temporary) (Class TU) visa because a bogus bank document had supported the applicant’s earlier Temporary Activity visa. The Tribunal accepted the document was bogus but found the applicant was unaware of it and neither complicit in nor recklessly indifferent to her coach’s or agent’s conduct. It set aside the refusal and remitted the matter with a direction that PIC 4020 is met for cl 500.217(1).
Key holding

For PIC 4020(1), where a third party supplies a bogus document, an applicant may be taken to have caused it to be given if complicit in, or recklessly indifferent to, that third party’s unlawful or dishonest conduct.

If the applicant is complicit in the provision of false information or 'indifferent' to a third party acting unlawfully or dishonestly, it can be said that the applicant caused it to be given.

TUNTIPONGMANEEKUL (Migration)

29 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2410Tribunal reviewVisa integrityVisa refusal reviewMigration
The applicant sought review of a delegate’s refusal of a Student (Temporary) (Class TU) visa under cl 500.217(1) because her partner visa application disclosed a de facto relationship from 5 January 2021 that had not been declared in her earlier student visa application. The Tribunal found she was in a de facto relationship before the student visa application and that the alleged migration agent error was not established. It affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

PIC 4020 is engaged where there is an element of fraud or deception by some person, although the applicant need not have known that the information was purposely untrue.

While PIC 4020 refers to information that is false, in the sense of purposely untrue, it is not necessary for the Minister (or the Tribunal on review) to conclude that the applicant was aware the information was purposely untrue in order for PIC 4020 to be engaged. However, an element of fraud or deception by some person is necessary to attract the operation of the provision: Trivedi v MIBP [2014] FCAFC 42.

The Owners - Strata Plan No 87231 v 3A Composites GmbH (No 10) [2026] FCA 351 (27 March 2026)

27 Mar 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 351Visa integrity
This was a Part IVA representative proceeding by the Shore and Five Dock owners corporations against 3A Composites GmbH (German manufacturer) and Halifax Vogel Group Pty Ltd (Australian supplier) concerning Alucobond PE and Alucobond Plus cladding. The applicants alleged the products were not of acceptable/merchantable quality under ACL s 54/TPA s 74D and that marketing was misleading under ACL ss 29, 33/TPA ss 53, 55.
Key holding

Whether cladding products are safe and BCA-compliant depends on individual assessment of the specific building; a product not inherently defective does not breach the consumer guarantee.

where legislative framework and expert evidence demonstrates safety and compliance of products can only be determined through individual assessment of specific buildings - Where products not inherently defective - claim not established.

SRFX and Minister for Immigration, Citizenship and Multicultural Affairs (Citizenship)

8 Oct 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3549Tribunal reviewVisa integrityVisa refusal reviewCharacter testMigration
SRFX applied for Australian citizenship by conferral, but a delegate refused approval under s 24(1) of the Australian Citizenship Act 2007 (Cth), finding he did not satisfy the good character requirement in s 21(2)(h) and that his identity could not be established under s 24(3). The Tribunal reviewed the decision, relying on the applicant’s migration history, prior partner visa findings and allegedly bogus documents, and affirmed the refusal. The case turns on whether the Tribunal could be satisfied of his good character and identity.
Key holding

In assessing good character for citizenship, the decision-maker must make a holistic assessment of the person’s enduring moral qualities, and a single sufficiently serious adverse incident can outweigh otherwise good qualities.

The decision-maker must look holistically at applicant's behaviour over time and reach a conclusion about the person's enduring moral qualities. Notwithstanding the obligation to take a holistic approach, it is established that a single adverse incident of sufficient weight and seriousness can outweigh many good qualities in a person.

Under PIC 4020(4), PIC 4020(1) and (2) may be waived only if there are compelling circumstances affecting Australia’s interests or compassionate or compelling circumstances affecting an Australian citizen, permanent resident or eligible New Zealand citizen that justify granting the visa.

2411742 (Refugee)

27 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 4375Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa refusal reviewVisa integrityMigration
Before: Damian Creedon
The applicant is an older South Korean citizen who claimed protection in Australia because he feared being unable to find work in South Korea due to age discrimination and long absence. The Tribunal affirmed the refusal of his protection visa, finding South Korea offers effective protection against age discrimination and that his economic/employment concerns did not meet protection criteria.
Key holding

Effective protection measures available from the receiving State can negate a well-founded fear of persecution under s 5J(2).

The Tribunal finds, therefore, that South Korea can offer effective protection to its citizens against age discrimination. It follows, and the Tribunal finds, that effective protection measures against age discrimination are available to the applicant in South Korea, and that therefore he does not have a well-founded fear of persecution on that ground: s 5J(2).
