Chalker v Baldwin [No 3]

15 Oct 2026Supreme Court of Victoria[2026] VSC 670Negligence
Before: Tsalamandris J
In Chalker v Baldwin [No 3] [2026] VSC 670, a self-represented plaintiff in a medical treatment and assault proceeding sought further discovery from Barwon Health, leave to issue subpoenas, and an extension of time to join a third defendant. Tsalamandris J largely confined discovery to documents relevant to the pleaded issues, rejected broad or peripheral categories, refused the Victoria Police subpoena, and refused the joinder extension.
Key holding

Further discovery is confined to documents relevant to the issues as pleaded, and requests that are peripheral or disproportionate to the central issues will not be ordered.

Accordingly, demands for discovery of documents which are peripheral to the central issues cannot be entertained. The Court is obliged to focus on the central issues as best it can be determined at this point in the litigation.

Caller v State of NSW

13 Oct 2026New South Wales Court of Appeal[2026] NSWCA 212Negligence
Before: Leeming JA at [1];Stern JA at [104];Ball JA at [105] Decision: 1. Appeal dismiss
The appellant, a former NSW police officer, suffered PTSD from cumulative exposure to traumatic incidents during service. The primary judge found the State breached its duty by failing to implement its own record-keeping system to monitor cumulative trauma exposure, but held the breach did not cause the PTSD. The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal, upholding the no-causation finding and rejecting challenges to other alleged breaches.
Key holding

In a negligence claim, the plaintiff must prove not only breach of duty but also that the breach caused the loss claimed.

In order to succeed on the negligence claim brought by the appellant, it was necessary for him to establish that there was a breach of duty owed to him which caused the loss (in this case, his developing PTSD).

Woolnough v Whittlesea City Council & Anor [2026] VSC 190 (7 April 2026)

7 Apr 2026Supreme Court of Victoria[2026] VSC 190Negligence
In Woolnough v Whittlesea City Council & Anor [2026] VSC 190, a young cricketer suffered catastrophic injuries after falling while vaulting a boundary fence at a council reserve during training. The Supreme Court of Victoria held the Council negligent for failing to install a pedestrian gate adjacent to the new nets, but found the Cricket Club not negligent and reduced damages by 20% for contributory negligence.
Key holding

In occupier negligence, a failure to install a modest pedestrian gate can be unreasonable where the occupier knows or ought to know that many users will foreseeably and repeatedly jump a boundary fence at that location, because the aggregate risk of injury—not merely the risk of a single jump—is relevant to the reasonableness calculus.

Further, and although, as noted, for most reasonably fit people, climbing or jumping over a fence on a single occasion carries only a very small risk of injury, the risk of injury is much greater when these single occasions are aggregated, as would be the case if, for example, every member of every football team were to jump or climb over the fence before and after each game and training session over multiple seasons.
