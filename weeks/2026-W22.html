2109620 (Refugee)

31 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2309Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
The applicant, a Malaysian citizen, sought review of a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa. His original application contained fabricated anti-Rohingya/anti-Islamic claims prepared by an unqualified agent, but he later claimed protection as a gay man fearing persecution in Malaysia. The Tribunal accepted his credibility and remitted the matter with a direction that he meets s 36(2)(a) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth).
Key holding

Under s 367A, the Tribunal should not draw an adverse credibility inference from claims first raised late where the applicant provides a reasonable explanation, such as agent fraud and acute fear of disclosing sexual identity.

I consider the combination of these factors does provide a reasonable explanation for why he did not raise his genuine claims at any point when his case was before the Department. I therefore draw no adverse views regarding the applicant's credibility due to his sexuality-related claims not being placed before the Department.

2544449 (Refugee)

29 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2395Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
In 2544449 (Refugee) [2026] ARTA 2395, the Tribunal reviewed a refusal of a protection visa to an Indian national who claimed to be a Hindu follower of Jagat Guru Rampal Ji Maharaj and feared religious extremists and police inaction. The Tribunal accepted his identity and some low-level private adherence but rejected his claimed public profile and past harm due to inconsistency and lack of evidence. It affirmed the refusal, finding neither the refugee nor complementary protection criteria were met.
Key holding

Harm caused by separation from a spouse when it arises from removal from Australia, rather than an intentional act or omission by a State or non-State actor in the receiving country, does not constitute “significant harm” for complementary protection under s 36(2A).

I find that the harm associated with the separation of the applicant from his wife, where the claimed harm arises from the act of removal from Australia to India rather than as a result of an intentional act or omission by a State or non-State actor within India, does not meet the definition of 'significant harm' in s 36(2A).

2217136 (Refugee)

29 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2329Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa to a Timorese applicant who claimed he could not return because of a moneylender debt and threats, plus general crime and socio-economic hardship. The loan claim was raised late and unsupported, and the general conditions were found to affect the population at large. The Tribunal affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

General socio-economic conditions and crime affecting the population at large, without personal targeting, do not qualify as persecution for the refugee criterion.

Furthermore, these other claims were claims about the general nature of life and living in Timor-Leste and would be applicable to the population generally and not directed or targeted at the applicant specifically and therefore would not qualify as persecutorial.

2004494 (Refugee)

29 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2303Tribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal reviewed the refusal of a Protection (Class XA) Subclass 866 visa for a young girl born in Australia to Malaysian citizen parents. It accepted that she is stateless, born out of wedlock, and unable to register her birth or readily obtain Malaysian citizenship. The Tribunal found she faces a real chance of serious harm in Malaysia and remitted the matter with a direction that she satisfies s 36(2)(a).
Key holding

A stateless child born out of wedlock in Malaysia may meet the refugee criterion where discriminatory citizenship and registration laws, combined with social stigma, cause serious harm for reasons of nationality and membership of a particular social group, without effective state protection.

I find that the applicant faces a real chance of serious harm for the essential and significant reasons of her nationality, or lack thereof, and because of her membership of the particular social group 'children born out of wedlock'. This characteristic is an innate or immutable characteristic, and it is not a fear of persecution.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
