2305663 (Refugee)

8 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2427Tribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa to a Malaysian man claiming fear of return because of his non-heterosexual identity and imputed Muslim background. 2303830 (Refugee)

8 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2411Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
This was a protection visa review for a Malaysian mother and her child, with the mother claiming fear of her former husband after serious domestic violence in Australia. The Tribunal accepted past family violence and threatening messages but found no objective real chance of future harm in Malaysia and that effective state protection was available. It affirmed both refusal decisions.
Key holding

A subjective fear of persecution is insufficient for refugee status unless the applicant also shows an objective real chance of persecution in the reasonably foreseeable future.

Therefore, while the Tribunal is prepared to accept that the applicant subjectively fears persecution from her ex-husband on return to Malaysia, the Tribunal was not satisfied that the objective requirement was met because 3.5 years has elapsed, the applicant is now divorced from her husband who is without means or connections to track her down and there is no real chance he will cause the applicants any harm in the reasonably foreseeable future.

Their son is politically active and of adverse interest to Egyptian authorities, and the Tribunal found the parents would be identified and questioned because of their relationship to him. Where authorities would question applicants solely because of their relationship to a son of adverse political interest, the imputed political opinion can found a well-founded fear, and such questioning may constitute serious harm in their particular circumstances.

The Tribunal finds that in the particular circumstances of the applicants, questioning by the authorities amounts to serious harm. The reason for this harm is the political opinion imputed to the applicants by the authorities, given their relationship to their son [Mr A].

2006580 (Refugee)

8 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2271Tribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a subclass 866 protection visa to an Iranian citizen who claims to have converted from Islam to Christianity in Australia. It accepted his core claims, found Iran would not protect him as a Christian convert, set aside the refusal and remitted the matter for reconsideration under s 36(2)(a) of the Migration Act.
Key holding

Where an applicant engages in religious conduct in Australia after arrival, s 5J(6) requires that conduct to be disregarded for the refugee assessment unless the applicant proves it was not done to strengthen the refugee claim; a genuine, sustained conversion supported by evidence can satisfy that requirement.

Section 5J(6) provides that any conduct engaged in by a person in Australia must be disregarded in determining whether the person has a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, or membership of a particular social group or political opinion, unless the person satisfies the decision maker that he or she engaged in the conduct otherwise than for the purpose of strengthening the claim to be a refugee.

2204790 (Refugee)

7 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2424Tribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
This was a review of a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa to a Saudi national who claims a well-founded fear of persecution for political opinion arising from his opposition to the Saudi regime. The Tribunal accepted the claim, set aside the refusal and remitted the matter with an order that the applicant meets s 36(2)(a) of the Migration Act.
Key holding

A real chance of persecution for political opinion can be established where a genuine dissident would be likely to express his views impulsively on return and thereby attract adverse attention from the authorities.

Having regard to my observations and the evidence set out above, I am satisfied that there is a real chance that, if returned to Saudi Arabia, the applicant would express his views impulsively or unguardedly, including by using profane and intemperate language or by making statements about the authorities or the royal family of the kind he made at the hearing, in circumstances where doing so may attract adverse attention from the authorities.

2204508 (Refugee)

7 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2334Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
A Brazilian woman sought review of a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa, claiming fear of her biological father, her stepfather, general and gender-based violence, and harm to her mental health if returned to Brazil. The Tribunal affirmed the refusal, finding no real chance of serious harm for a Convention reason and no real risk of significant harm on complementary protection.
Key holding

For a national, refugee protection requires being outside their country and unable or unwilling to avail themselves of protection owing to a well-founded fear of persecution.

A person is a refugee if, in the case of a person who has a nationality, they are outside the country of their nationality and, owing to a well-founded fear of persecution, are unable or unwilling to avail themselves of the protection of that country: s 5H(1)(a).

2204301 (Refugee)

4 May 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2288Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
The applicant, a Philippines citizen, sought review of a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa, claiming fear of harm from her ex-husband and from Filipino workers involved in a failed recruitment scheme. The Administrative Review Tribunal affirmed the refusal, finding her evidence inconsistent, contradictory and changing and not accepting the claims at face value. The Tribunal was therefore not satisfied she met the refugee or complementary protection criteria.
Key holding

In assessing protection claims, the benefit of the doubt is available only where the applicant is generally credible and their statements are coherent, plausible and not contrary to known facts.

The benefit of the doubt should, however, only be given when all available evidence has been obtained and checked and when the examiner is satisfied as to the applicant's general credibility. The applicant's statements must be coherent and plausible and must not run counter to generally known facts.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
