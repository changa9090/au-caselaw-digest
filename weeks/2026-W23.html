2109695 (Refugee)

5 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2311Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal (ARTA) affirmed the refusal of a protection visa to a Tongan national who arrived in Australia as a minor and feared homelessness/economic hardship in Tonga due to having no family or support network. It accepted his circumstances but found no entity in Tonga would persecute him or cause significant harm. Ministerial intervention was mentioned but not recommended.
Key holding

Economic hardship or lack of support does not amount to persecution or significant harm unless an entity in the receiving country would cause that harm.

Each of the verbal phrases contained in the categories of harm which form the definition of 'significant harm' contemplate that there is an entity in the country concerned which would cause that harm; that is, 'will be arbitrarily deprived of', 'will be carried out on', and 'will be subjected to'. Because there is no entity which would seek to cause any of those categories of harm to the Applicant, he does not meet the statutory criterion for complementary protection.

2420008 (Refugee)

4 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2421Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
This is a Tribunal review of a delegate’s refusal of protection visas to a Malaysian husband and wife. The first applicant claimed harm arising from opposition to land acquisition and a proposed chemical factory, but the Tribunal found his claims lacked detail, were inconsistent and were not credible, and affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

Applicants bear responsibility for providing sufficient particulars and supporting evidence, and mere assertions of feared harm do not themselves establish a well-founded fear or real risk of significant harm.

It is well established that the mere assertions by an applicant that he or she fears persecution for particular reasons, such assertions do not of themselves establish the validity of the asserted fears, for the reasons claimed or that such fears are well-founded. Similarly, such claims, that an applicant faces a real risk of significant harm do not of themselves establish that these risks exist or amount to a 'real risk' or significant harm.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
