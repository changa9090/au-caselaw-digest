2403487 (Refugee)

5 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2408Tribunal reviewMigration
The applicant, a Vietnamese national accepted as Kinh ethnicity, sought a protection visa claiming Catholic faith, anti-government political opinion, religious harm, debt-collector threats, economic hardship and mental health issues. The Tribunal decided the review without a hearing at the applicant’s request and affirmed the refusal, finding his claims vague and unsubstantiated and that he faced no real chance or real risk of serious or significant harm in Vietnam.
Key holding

The Tribunal may decide a review without a hearing where the applicant has requested that course and the issues for determination can be adequately determined in the absence of the parties.

These include that the applicant has requested the Tribunal make its decision without holding a hearing, and it appears to the Tribunal that the issues for determination in the proceeding can be adequately determined in the absence of the parties to the proceeding.

2109695 (Refugee)

5 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2311Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal (ARTA) affirmed the refusal of a protection visa to a Tongan national who arrived in Australia as a minor and feared homelessness/economic hardship in Tonga due to having no family or support network. It accepted his circumstances but found no entity in Tonga would persecute him or cause significant harm. Ministerial intervention was mentioned but not recommended.
Key holding

Economic hardship or lack of support does not amount to persecution or significant harm unless an entity in the receiving country would cause that harm.

Each of the verbal phrases contained in the categories of harm which form the definition of 'significant harm' contemplate that there is an entity in the country concerned which would cause that harm; that is, 'will be arbitrarily deprived of', 'will be carried out on', and 'will be subjected to'. Because there is no entity which would seek to cause any of those categories of harm to the Applicant, he does not meet the statutory criterion for complementary protection.

2420008 (Refugee)

4 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2421Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
This is a Tribunal review of a delegate’s refusal of protection visas to a Malaysian husband and wife. The first applicant claimed harm arising from opposition to land acquisition and a proposed chemical factory, but the Tribunal found his claims lacked detail, were inconsistent and were not credible, and affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

Applicants bear responsibility for providing sufficient particulars and supporting evidence, and mere assertions of feared harm do not themselves establish a well-founded fear or real risk of significant harm.

It is well established that the mere assertions by an applicant that he or she fears persecution for particular reasons, such assertions do not of themselves establish the validity of the asserted fears, for the reasons claimed or that such fears are well-founded. Similarly, such claims, that an applicant faces a real risk of significant harm do not of themselves establish that these risks exist or amount to a 'real risk' or significant harm.

2421723 (Refugee)

3 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2426Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: General Member T Griffin
The Tribunal reviewed a protection visa refusal for a Taiwanese applicant who claimed she had been extorted, threatened and assaulted by gangsters while working at street stalls, with police allegedly failing to help. The Tribunal accepted aspects of her past harassment and one assault but found no Convention reason and no real chance of serious harm or real risk of significant harm on return. It affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

A residual possibility of general crime, without more, does not establish a well-founded fear of persecution.

Like most citizens of Taiwan, there remains a residual possibility of the applicant may again fall victim to crime in Taiwan. That potential however, does not place her within the definition of a well-founded fear of persecution under the Act.

2434629 (Refugee)

2 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2422Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
This is a protection visa review for a Chinese citizen who claimed to fear harm for political opinion said to be critical of the Chinese government, including claimed online posts, police assault and threats from an underground organisation. She arrived in Australia in May 2022, applied for protection in July 2024, declined a hearing and asked the Tribunal to decide on the papers. Key holding

Under s 106(3) of the ART Act, the Tribunal may proceed without a hearing only where the applicant’s request is informed by notice of the issues and consequences and a reasonable opportunity to present their case.

This means I must ensure that the applicant is not "in the dark" about the issues to be determined and the consequences of those issues being determined without a hearing before I then do determine this case in the absence of a hearing.

2209726 (Refugee)

2 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2338Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa to a Cambodian national who claimed fear from gangsters and from political activism/social media criticism of the Cambodian government. It accepted a 2018 gangster incident but found no continuing interest, and rejected the political/social media claims on credibility grounds. The Tribunal affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

The real risk threshold for complementary protection is the same as the real chance threshold for refugee protection.

The Tribunal notes threshold for the 'real risk' element in the complementary protection criterion in s 36(2)(aa) is the same as that for the 'real chance' test in the refugee criterion in s 36(2)(a).

The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a Protection visa to a Dinka man raised in Australia, who feared removal to South Sudan and [Country 1]. 2208072 (Refugee)

2 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2302Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
In 2208072 (Refugee) [2026] ARTA 2302, the Tribunal affirmed refusal of a protection visa to a Tongan applicant who initially claimed cyclone Harold damage but at hearing relied on economic hardship, no place to stay in Tonga, and fear of drugs. The Tribunal found no real chance of Convention-related serious harm or real risk of significant harm. It also found no family-unit criterion because his mother is an Australian permanent resident, not a protection visa holder.
Key holding

Claims of economic hardship arising from unemployment and lack of accommodation do not fall within the refugee criterion in s 5J absent a real chance of serious harm for a Convention reason.

Instead, his claims are that he would suffer economic hardship if he returned to Tonga since he will not have a job or place to live. Those grounds do not fall within the scope of s 5J of the Act.

2305310 (Migration)

2 Jun 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2301Tribunal reviewMigration
The Tribunal affirmed refusal of a Subclass 820 Partner (Temporary) (Class UK) visa because the applicant and sponsor were not in a de facto relationship at the time of visa application. Although they had a romantic boyfriend-girlfriend relationship and registered their relationship, they never lived together and lacked sufficient financial, household and commitment indicia under s 5CB. The sponsor had notified relationship breakdown and family violence was raised, but the Tribunal did not reach it.
Key holding

A romantic or boyfriend-girlfriend relationship, relationship registration or limited social recognition does not establish a de facto relationship for partner visa purposes; the applicant must satisfy s 5CB(2), including mutual commitment to a shared life to the exclusion of all others at the relevant time.

However, the mere existence of a romantic relationship is not sufficient to meet the requirements for a partner visa in question. There must have been a de facto relationship as defined in s 5CB of the Act.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
