Yanunijarra Aboriginal Corporation RNTBC v State of Western Australia [2026] FCAFC 7 (12 February 2026)

12 Feb 2026Federal Court of Australia (Full Court)[2026] FCAFC 7Migration: court challenge
The Full Court considered whether the State’s inclusion of an expedited procedure statement in notices under s 29 of the Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) is a reviewable decision under the ADJR Act or otherwise amenable to judicial review. The primary judge had summarily dismissed the native title parties’ challenge, following Holt v Manzie. The Full Court granted leave to appeal but dismissed the appeal.
Key holding

The inclusion of an expedited procedure statement in a s 29 NTA notice is not a reviewable decision under the ADJR Act or otherwise amenable to judicial review because the statutory provisions operate on the fact the notice includes the statement, not on any anterior Government party decision or opinion.

His Honour concluded that the inclusion of an expedited procedure statement in a notice given under s 29 is not reviewable under the ADJR Act or otherwise amenable to judicial review.

Luong v Minister for Immigration, Citizenship and Multicultural Affairs [2026] FCAFC 6 (11 February 2026)

11 Feb 2026Federal Court of Australia (Full Court)[2026] FCAFC 6Migration: court challenge
The appellant, a Vietnamese citizen, had his visa mandatorily cancelled under s 501(3A) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) after a serious violent offence. The AAT later revoked that cancellation, but the Minister personally set aside the AAT decision and cancelled the visa under s 501BA on national-interest grounds. The Full Court dismissed the appellant’s appeal from the primary judge’s refusal of judicial review.
Key holding

The national interest under s 501BA is broad and unconfined, and the Act imposes no mandatory factors on the Minister’s national-interest determination.

The Act does not stipulate any factors to which the Minister must have regard in determining what is in the national interest for the purposes of s 501BA of the Act. What is in the national interest does not readily lend itself to the compartmentalisation of the considerations involved, and it would be contrary to principle for the words "in the national interest" to be given a confined meaning.

Spaleta v Secretary, Department of Social Services [2026] FCAFC 5 (11 February 2026)

11 Feb 2026Federal Court of Australia (Full Court)[2026] FCAFC 5Tribunal reviewVisa nomination
This was a Full Court appeal about a disability support pension claim. The appellant challenged the AAT’s finding that her conditions were not fully treated or stabilised, so she could not get 20 impairment points under s 94(1)(b) of the Social Security Act 1991 (Cth). The Full Court allowed the appeal, holding that “reasonable treatment” under the Impairment Tables Determination requires consideration of whether treatment is affordable to the claimant, and remitted the matter.
Key holding

In assessing whether treatment is “reasonable treatment” under s 6(7) of the Impairment Tables Determination, “reasonable cost” requires consideration of whether the treatment is reasonably affordable to the claimant in their circumstances, not merely whether the cost is objectively reasonable in the market.

A question arises as to whether the reference to "reasonable cost" in s 6(7)(b) is to the objective reasonableness of the cost of the treatment, or to the different question whether the treatment is reasonably affordable to a claimant in the position of the person concerned. In our view, the latter is the better construction having regard to the context and purpose of the provision.

Zhu v Minister for Immigration, Citizenship, Migrant Services and Multicultural Affairs (No 2) [2026] FCAFC 4 (10 February 2026)

10 Feb 2026Federal Court of Australia (Full Court)[2026] FCAFC 4Migration: court challenge
In this Full Court costs judgment following dismissal of Mr Zhu’s migration appeal, the Court determined who should pay the costs of the primary proceeding. Mr Zhu sought no costs order on public interest grounds, or alternatively costs fixed under the migration scale in Sch 2; the Ministers sought costs as agreed or taxed. The Court ordered Mr Zhu to pay the first and third respondents’ costs of proceeding BRG193/2022 as agreed or taxed.
Key holding

In a migration proceeding where a party applies for costs under r 22.02, the Court has a discretion to choose a method under r 22.02(2) instead of Sch 2 scale costs if that method is more appropriate.

In a migration proceeding, if an application is made under r 22.02, then the Court must exercise its discretion to determine whether one of the other options in r 22.02(2) is more appropriate than the application of the Schedule 2 scale costs pursuant to r 29.13(1); see r 29.13(3).

Lockhart v Bartholomai & Anor (No. 2) [2026] QDC 1 (10 February 2026)

10 Feb 2026District Court of Queensland[2026] QDC 1Negligence
Lockhart v Bartholomai & Anor (No. 2) is a Queensland District Court costs decision following dismissal of the plaintiff’s personal injuries claim. The defendants sought indemnity costs from the dates of a PIPA mandatory final offer and a UCPR offer, arguing the plaintiff’s refusal of both was imprudent. The court refused indemnity costs and ordered costs on the standard basis.
Key holding

A refused settlement offer does not justify indemnity costs unless the refusal was imprudent; the risk of liability does not make refusal imprudent where the offers were substantially less than the damages the plaintiff could reasonably have expected when the offers were active.

I am satisfied, albeit with some hesitation, that the risk of liability was not so great as to overwhelm the fact that the discrepancy between the defendants' offers and the amount the plaintiff could have reasonably expected to have been awarded at the time they were active means that the refusal of them was not imprudent.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
