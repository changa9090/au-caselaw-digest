1 Sep 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 4185Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: Hee-Jung Kim
The AATA reviewed a delegate’s refusal of protection visas to a Nepali family—husband, wife and their Australian-born daughter—who claimed fear of harm in Nepal because of their inter-caste/inter-religious marriage. Terrones Tafur (Migration)

31 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3967Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationMigration
Before: Christine Kannis
The Tribunal reviewed cancellation of a Subclass 500 (Student) visa because the first applicant was not enrolled in a full-time registered course from 20 January 2022 to 5 January 2023. It affirmed cancellation, finding breach of condition 8202(2)(a) and no sufficient discretionary reasons against cancellation. It also held it had no jurisdiction over the secondary applicants, whose visas were automatically cancelled under s 140(1).
Key holding

Once a breach of condition 8202 grounds cancellation under s 116(1)(b) and cancellation is not mandatory under s 116(3), the Tribunal must proceed to exercise the discretion whether to cancel.

For these reasons, the Tribunal is satisfied that the ground for cancellation in s 116(1)(b) exists. As that ground does not require mandatory cancellation under s 116(3), the Tribunal must proceed to consider whether to exercise its discretion to cancel the visa.

Movono (Migration)

31 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3916Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationMigration
Before: Christine Kannis
Miss Make Liku Movono held a Subclass 500 (Student) visa. A delegate cancelled it because she was not enrolled in a full-time registered course from 9 August 2021, breaching condition 8202(2)(a). The Tribunal reviewed the cancellation and affirmed it, finding significant non-enrolment and no extenuating or compassionate circumstances.
Key holding

A breach of student visa condition 8202(2)(a) by failing to be enrolled in a full-time registered course establishes the discretionary cancellation ground under s 116(1)(b), which is not mandatory under s 116(3) and therefore requires consideration of the discretion to cancel.

For these reasons, the Tribunal is satisfied that the ground for cancellation in s 116(1)(b) exists. As that ground does not require mandatory cancellation under s 116(3), the Tribunal must proceed to consider whether to exercise its discretion to cancel the visa.

Khendo Sherpa (Migration)

31 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3393Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: Christine Kannis
The Tribunal affirmed cancellation of Ms Dawa Khendo Sherpa’s Subclass 500 (Student) visa (Class TU) because she was not enrolled in a full-time registered course from 11 March 2021 to 8 June 2023. The Tribunal found breach of condition 8202(2)(a) and, after considering discretionary factors, declined to set aside cancellation.
Key holding

Condition 8202(2)(a) imposes a continuing requirement that a student visa holder remain enrolled in a full-time registered course, so even a temporary gap in enrolment breaches the condition.

Condition 8202(2)(a) requires that the holder be enrolled and the enrolment requirement is a continuing requirement and does not allow the visa holder to cease to be enrolled in a course, even to the extent of a temporary gap in enrolment.

Lin (Migration)

31 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3390Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationMigration
Before: Christine Kannis
The Tribunal affirmed cancellation of Mr Lin’s Class TU (Subclass 500) student visa because he was not enrolled in a full-time registered course from 19 November 2021 to 12 March 2023. He claimed a former migration agent failed to pay course fees, but the Tribunal found no probative evidence and held he remained responsible for maintaining enrolment. The cancellation was affirmed on discretion.
Key holding

A student visa holder is personally responsible for understanding and complying with visa conditions, including maintaining enrolment and checking that tuition fees have been paid, and reliance on a migration agent does not excuse non-compliance.

The Tribunal notes that even if the applicant had an agent who assisted him, that does not diminish his own responsibility to check that his fees have been paid. In the Tribunal's view it is the responsibility of a visa holder to be aware of the conditions of their visa and remain compliant with them.

2405987 (Refugee)

30 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 4154Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: Khanh Hoang
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal of a protection visa to a Vanuatu citizen who claimed family land inheritance disputes, political tensions and black magic threats. It accepted some family tension and threats but found he could safely return to Port Vila, where he had lived for about a decade, and affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

A well-founded fear of persecution requires a real chance that is not remote or far-fetched, and the chance may be below 50 per cent.

A 'real chance' is one that is not remote or insubstantial or a far-fetched possibility. A person can have a well-founded fear of persecution even though the possibility of the persecution occurring is well below 50 per cent: Chan Yee Kin v MIEA (1989) 169 CLR 379.

Araoz (Migration)

30 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3174Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: David Barker
Mrs Claudia Araoz sought review of a delegate’s refusal of a Student (Temporary) (Class TU) visa. Although the delegate refused for not being a genuine temporary entrant, the Tribunal considered whether she met the current enrolment requirement. Because she did not respond to Tribunal invitations and PRISMS showed no current Confirmation of Enrolment, the Tribunal affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

The Tribunal treats PRISMS as a reliable record of a visa applicant’s enrolment unless there is specific evidence to the contrary.

2429454 (Migration)

30 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3172Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: Anne Grant
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s refusal to grant a Bridging E (Class WE) visa, where the applicant wanted to remain in Australia for criminal hearings and relied on having acceptable arrangements to depart. The Tribunal was not satisfied he met cl 050.212(2) or any other alternative criterion, and affirmed the refusal. It also found he did not meet Subclass 051 criteria.
Key holding

For cl 050.212(2), a bare assertion of third-party financial assistance, without evidence of the supporter’s willingness and capacity, is insufficient to establish acceptable arrangements to depart Australia.

A simple assertion that someone will help with flight purchases and financial support, without any confirmation demonstrating that the supporting person is willing and has the capacity to do so is insufficient, in my view, to be considered 'appropriate arrangements' for departure.

Jiang (Migration)

28 Aug 2024Administrative Review Tribunal[2024] AATA 3198Tribunal reviewVisa refusal reviewMigration
Before: Tania Flood
The Tribunal affirmed refusal of Ms Jiang’s Visitor (Class FA) Subclass 600 visa because she applied after her substantive visa expired. It found the late application resulted from forgetfulness and inadequate reminders, not factors beyond her control under Schedule 3 criterion 3004(c). The applicant was also offshore at the time of decision, so cl 600.411 was not met.
Key holding

Under criterion 3004(c), whether the applicant ceased to hold a substantive visa because of factors beyond their control is assessed subjectively and practically, and forgetfulness or failure to take available compliance measures will not ordinarily qualify.

The test is in that sense "subjective" rather than being directed at deciding what would have been beyond the control of an abstract or "reasonable" person. A second point is that what is "beyond control" should be tested by considering whether the happening of the event was or was not within the control of the applicant in a practical or realistic sense.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
