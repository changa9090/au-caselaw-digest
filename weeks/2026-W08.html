Mr Peter Rennick v Victorian WorkCover Authority [2026] FWC 554 (20 February 2026)

20 Feb 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 554Employment & workplace
Mr Peter Rennick, a WorkSafe Victoria inspector of 26 years, was dismissed on 8 July 2025 after PTSD left him unable to perform his substantive inspector role. The Fair Work Commission found the dismissal was not unfair because the medical evidence showed a valid reason based on total and likely permanent incapacity. The unfair dismissal application was dismissed.
Key holding

An employee’s incapacity to perform the inherent requirements of their role at the date of dismissal can provide a valid reason for dismissal under s 387(a).

I find that Mr Rennick's incapacity to perform the inherent requirements of his job is a valid reason for his dismissal from employment.

Matthew Holmes v Torbay Constructions Pty Ltd [2026] FWC 552 (20 February 2026)

20 Feb 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 552Employment & workplace
Matthew Holmes performed building and site management work for Torbay Constructions from late 2019 until 10 September 2025, invoicing through his business name. Torbay Constructions objected to his s.365 general protections dismissal application on the basis that he was an independent contractor, not an employee. The Commission found Mr Holmes was an employee at the time of dismissal and dismissed the jurisdictional objection.
Key holding

A court or tribunal determining employment status must look to the real substance of the relationship; contractual labels cannot change its true nature.

The terms and terminology of the contract are always important. However, the parties cannot alter the true nature of their relationship by putting a different label on it.

United Workers' Union v Olex Australia Pty Ltd [2026] FWC 536 (20 February 2026)

20 Feb 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 536Employment & workplace

The Fair Work Commission determined the matter on the papers and granted the PABO because it was satisfied the UWU had been and was genuinely trying to reach an agreement.
Key holding

The FWC must not conflate the good faith bargaining obligations under s.228 with the s.443(1)(b) requirement that the applicant has been, and is, genuinely trying to reach an enterprise agreement.

Magri v Zeebax Studios Pty Ltd & Anor (Ruling) [2026] VCC 143 (20 February 2026)

20 Feb 2026County Court of Victoria[2026] VCC 143Negligence
Following a jury verdict, Ms Magri succeeded against her employer Zeebax Studios but failed against Torrington Superannuation, the landlord/occupier. The County Court ruled on costs, refusing Bullock and Sanderson orders and requiring Zeebax to pay Torrington’s costs on an indemnity basis from expiry of a Calderbank offer. Torrington was ordered to bear its own reserved costs, and Ms Magri cannot recover costs from Zeebax because of s344(2) of the Workplace Injury Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 2013.
Key holding

A Bullock or Sanderson order will not ordinarily be made unless the claims against the defendants are interdependent or essentially alternative and it is reasonable for the plaintiff to have joined the successful defendant, with the unsuccessful defendant's conduct making the order just.

In short an order will not ordinarily be made unless: (a) the plaintiff's claims against the two defendants are interdependent or essentially alternative claims; and (b) it is reasonable for the plaintiff to have joined the successful defendant and the conduct of the unsuccessful defendant has been such as to make the order just.

RXY v Mental Health Tribunal & Anor [2026] VSC 71 (20 February 2026)

20 Feb 2026Supreme Court of Victoria[2026] VSC 71Migration: court challenge
RXY sought leave to appeal to the Supreme Court of Victoria from a VCAT decision affirming two Mental Health Tribunal decisions: denying access to documents and making a community treatment order. By the time of judgment, the community treatment order had expired and been replaced, and the applicant sought declaratory relief rather than any order affecting the existing order. The Court refused leave to appeal because the proposed appeal and declarations would have no foreseeable or practical consequences.
Key holding

A court may refuse leave to appeal under s 148 where the proposed appeal and declaratory relief would have no foreseeable or practical consequences, even if the proposed grounds have some public utility.

Hence, declaratory relief must be directed to the determination of legal controversies and not to answering abstract or hypothetical questions. The person seeking relief must have 'a real interest' and relief will not be granted if the question 'is purely hypothetical', if relief is 'claimed in relation to circumstances that [have] not occurred and might never happen' or if 'the Court's declaration will produce no foreseeable consequences for the parties'.

Hemingway v Kennedy [2026] VSC 51 (20 February 2026)

20 Feb 2026Supreme Court of Victoria[2026] VSC 51Negligence
The Supreme Court of Victoria restrained Scott Legal from acting for both defendants in a tort proceeding brought by Mark Hemingway against Meg and Heidi Kennedy. The plaintiff alleges the first defendant falsely accused him of rape and that the defendants conspired by unlawful means in connection with his family law access to his children. Both defendants were represented by the same firm, and the plaintiff sought an order under the Court’s inherent jurisdiction to restrain that joint representation.
Key holding

Where one co-defendant is alleged to have exercised overbearing influence over another in fraudulent conduct, joint representation of both creates a real risk of compromising the integrity and appearance of the judicial process, which may warrant restraining the solicitors from acting for both.

Where, as here, there is an allegation that one party has had an overbearing influence on the conduct of another resulting in conduct of a fraudulent character, there is a real risk that the integrity of the judicial process - and its appearance - is likely to be compromised if the parties are jointly represented.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
