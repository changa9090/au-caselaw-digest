EEK25 v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

8 Oct 2026Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia[2026] FedCFamC2G 1960Migration: court challengeTribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
EEK25 sought judicial review under s 476 of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) of an Administrative Review Tribunal decision affirming refusal of a protection (subclass 866) visa. The Tribunal rejected her claims of harm from loan sharks based on credibility findings and DFAT country information. The Federal Circuit and Family Court dismissed the application, finding no jurisdictional error.
Key holding

A judicial review ground that is not particularised may be dismissed on that basis alone.

A "failure to particularise a ground of review is itself a sufficient basis to dismiss it": NWWJ v Minister for Immigration, Citizenship, Migrant Services and Multicultural Affairs [2020] FCAFC 176 at [37].

FCP20 v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

7 Oct 2026Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia[2026] FedCFamC2G 1974Migration: court challengeTribunal reviewVisa nominationMigration
FCP20, a Pakistani Shia applicant, sought judicial review under s 476 of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) of an Administrative Appeals Tribunal decision affirming refusal of a Protection (Class XA) (Subclass 866) visa. He alleged jurisdictional error arising from the Tribunal’s treatment of requested witnesses, his claims and medical evidence, and its compliance with ss 424AA and 424A. The Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (Division 2) dismissed the application and ordered costs.
Key holding

Information provided by an applicant during the process leading to the reviewable decision falls within the s 424A(3)(ba) exception, so the Tribunal need not give particulars or invite comment under ss 424A or 424AA.

However, the adverse information concerning the expiry of the applicant's first student visa, the grant of his second student visa and his delay in seeking protection appeared in material provided by the applicant during the visa application process, including his passport, visa application and written statement of claims. Accordingly, I find the information is within the exception in s 424A(3)(ba) of the Act and therefore the Tribunal's obligations under ss 424A or 424AA did not arise.

DHZ23 v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

2 Oct 2026Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia[2026] FedCFamC2G 1942Migration: court challengeTribunal reviewVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewMigration
The applicant sought judicial review under s 476 of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) of a Tribunal decision affirming refusal of a Protection (subclass 866) visa. The Tribunal rejected the applicant’s claims concerning forced demolition, compensation and alleged past harm, finding no well-founded fear of persecution or real risk of significant harm. The Court found no jurisdictional error and dismissed the application with fixed costs.
Key holding

Mere disagreement with a Tribunal’s factual findings does not establish jurisdictional error on judicial review.

To the extent that the Applicant's submissions amount to disagreement with the Tribunal's findings, mere disagreement does not establish jurisdictional error: see Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs v Eshetu [1999] HCA 21; (1999) 197 CLR 611 at [40] per Gleeson CJ and McHugh J and Vo v Minister for Home Affairs [2019] FCAFC 108; (2019) 269 FCR 566 per Derrington, Banks-Smith and Colvin JJ.

Chen v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

2 Oct 2026Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia[2026] FedCFamC2G 1939Migration: court challengeTribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa nominationMigration
The applicant sought judicial review under s 476 of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) of a Tribunal decision affirming cancellation of her student visa under s 116(1)(g), based on her conviction for concealing a serious indictable offence. She alleged the Tribunal constructively failed to consider claims about low reoffending risk and respect for immigration law, and exceeded its function by going beyond the District Court conviction and sentence. The Court dismissed the application with costs.
Key holding

A decision-maker may consider conduct beyond the elements of the offence, but cannot rely on evidence contrary to the essential facts establishing the conviction or sentence where that conviction or sentence is the jurisdictional foundation.

I concur with the Minister's submission that the ratio of this case is that where the conviction or sentence is the foundation for the decision maker's jurisdiction, it would be an error for the Tribunal to rely upon evidence that is contrary to the essential conviction or sentencing facts: HZCP at [78].

Adil v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

2 Oct 2026Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia[2026] FedCFamC2G 1926Migration: court challengeVisa integrityMigration
The applicant sought judicial review under s 476 of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) of an Administrative Appeals Tribunal decision refusing him a student visa on the basis that he had breached PIC 4020(1) by answering "No" to a question about previous visa refusals when he had in fact been refused twice. He ran four grounds: apprehended bias, non-compliance with the oral decision requirements in s 368D(2)(a), misunderstanding of his claims, and misconstruction of PIC 4020(1).
Key holding

PIC 4020(1) is only engaged where information is false or misleading in the "purposely untrue" sense involving fraud or deception rather than innocent mistake, and a tribunal need not use those express words provided its reasons disclose that it addressed the innocent-versus-purposeful distinction.

The Tribunal was not required to use the words "purposely untrue", provided it addressed the question as to whether the inaccurate information was innocently or purposely provided.
