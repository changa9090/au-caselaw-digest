Mishra and Minister for Immigration and Citizenship (Migration)

15 Oct 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2327Tribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa refusal reviewCharacter testMigration
The Tribunal reviewed a delegate’s decision not to revoke the mandatory cancellation of Mr Mishra’s Subclass 155 Five-Year Resident Return visa under s 501(3A) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth). Mr Mishra accepted he did not pass the character test because of a 22-month aggregate sentence for family violence offences. The Tribunal affirmed the non-revocation decision after considering whether another reason existed under s 501CA(4)(b)(ii) and Direction No. 110.
Key holding

Where a visa is mandatorily cancelled because the holder fails the character test due to a substantial criminal record, the Tribunal must consider whether there is another reason to revoke under s 501CA(4)(b)(ii), applying Direction No. 110.

In accordance with s 501CA(4)(b)(ii), the Tribunal must therefore consider whether there is another reason to exercise the discretion under s 501CA(4) to revoke the original decision.

Smith v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

15 Oct 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 1520Migration: court challengeTribunal reviewVisa cancellationVisa nominationVisa refusal reviewCharacter testMigration
Mr Smith, a New Zealand citizen, had his Class TY Subclass 444 visa cancelled under s 501(3A) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) after criminal convictions and imprisonment. A delegate refused to revoke the cancellation, and the Administrative Review Tribunal affirmed that refusal. On judicial review, the Federal Court found the Tribunal misconstrued Direction 110’s definition of “family violence” and set aside its decision.
Key holding

Conduct falling within an example of family violence in Direction 110 cl 4 is not automatically family violence; it must also have the effect of coercing or controlling a family member or causing that person to be fearful.

For example, behaviour which involves "intentionally damaging or destroying property" will not necessarily constitute family violence. It will only do so if that behaviour had the effect of coercing or controlling a member of the perpetrator's family, or causing the family member or members to be fearful.

Gautama v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

15 Oct 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 1519Migration: court challengeMigration
The applicant sought judicial review of an Administrative Review Tribunal decision affirming the non-revocation of his cancelled Five-Year Resident Return (Class BB) (Subclass 155) visa under s 501CA(4) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth). He argued the Tribunal erred by failing to apply the s 201 “10-year rule”, failing to consider mandatory considerations and misconstruing s 501CA(4)(b)(ii) as discretionary. The Federal Court dismissed the application.
Key holding

Section 201 of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth), the “10-year rule”, has no application to mandatory visa cancellation under s 501(3A), because s 201 belongs to the separate deportation framework.

I accept the Minister's submissions. The Tribunal was correct in its conclusion that s 201 had no application to the matter before it.

ROBB and Minister for Immigration and Citizenship (Migration)

The Tribunal affirmed a delegate’s decision not to revoke the mandatory cancellation of Mr Robb’s subclass 155 visa under s 501(3A) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth). Mr Robb accepted he failed the character test because of a substantial criminal record, and the Tribunal found the protection of the Australian community, family violence and community expectations outweighed his ties and rehabilitation claims. The case is a fact-specific Direction No. 110 review.
Key holding

Where an applicant fails the character test, the Tribunal can revoke a mandatory visa cancellation only if satisfied under s 501CA(4)(b)(ii) that there is another reason, assessed through Direction No. 110.

The statutory power to revoke will only be enlivened if there is 'another reason' why the Cancellation Decision should be revoked.

Iiga v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship [2026] FCA 234 (10 March 2026)

10 Mar 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 234Migration: court challenge
Mr Iiga sought judicial review of an Administrative Review Tribunal decision affirming the non-revocation of his mandatory visa cancellation under s 501(3A) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth). He alleged the Tribunal failed to comply with Direction 110 by not properly considering family violence seriousness and the risk of reoffending. The Federal Court dismissed the application with costs.
Key holding

A breach of a domestic violence order is only an act of “family violence” for Direction 110 if it involved threatened or actual violence, coercion/control, or fear; benign non-violent breaches are not enough.

As the definition is written, a breach of a domestic violence order will only be capable of constituting an act of "family violence" if it involved threatened or actual violence, had a coercive or controlling effect, or caused fear. If a contravention of a domestic violence order does not have that characteristic, it cannot be regarded as an act of "family violence" for the purposes of applying Direction 110.

February 2026 (1)

Luong v Minister for Immigration, Citizenship and Multicultural Affairs [2026] FCAFC 6 (11 February 2026)

11 Feb 2026Federal Court of Australia (Full Court)[2026] FCAFC 6Migration: court challenge
The appellant, a Vietnamese citizen, had his visa mandatorily cancelled under s 501(3A) of the Migration Act 1958 (Cth) after a serious violent offence. The AAT later revoked that cancellation, but the Minister personally set aside the AAT decision and cancelled the visa under s 501BA on national-interest grounds. The Full Court dismissed the appellant’s appeal from the primary judge’s refusal of judicial review.
Key holding

The national interest under s 501BA is broad and unconfined, and the Act imposes no mandatory factors on the Minister’s national-interest determination.

The Act does not stipulate any factors to which the Minister must have regard in determining what is in the national interest for the purposes of s 501BA of the Act. What is in the national interest does not readily lend itself to the compartmentalisation of the considerations involved, and it would be contrary to principle for the words "in the national interest" to be given a confined meaning.
