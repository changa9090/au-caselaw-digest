Mr Josateki Vunimasi & Mr Aminio Cava and Others v Woodward Foods Australia Pty Ltd & Mr Richard Tomasevic

23 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission (Full Bench)[2026] FWCFB 284Employment & workplaceContracts
This was a Full Bench appeal concerning seven PALM scheme workers whose s 365 general protections dismissal applications named Woodward Foods as respondent, although their actual employer/dismisser was Whales. The Deputy President found no valid s 365 application because they did not allege dismissal in contravention of Part 3-1, and the Full Bench refused permission to appeal. The appeal also sought to raise joint employment and host-employer oral promise arguments not argued at first instance.
Key holding

For a valid s 365 application, the person must have been dismissed and must allege that the dismissal contravened Part 3-1; otherwise the Commission’s s 368 powers are not enlivened.

It is abundantly clear that the Deputy President applied the principle outlined in Milford. He properly determined that the Appellants were not entitled to make applications under s 365 and nor were the functions of the Commission under s 368 enlivened because the second criterion was not met.

Aurecon Australasia Pty Ltd & Tristan Meo v Jennifer Baker

20 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission (Full Bench)[2026] FWCFB 283Employment & workplace
The Full Bench considered an appeal from a decision to issue a certificate under s 368(3) of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) despite Aurecon’s jurisdictional objection that Ms Baker had not been dismissed. The Full Bench held the Commissioner was required to determine that maintained objection before issuing the certificate. Permission to appeal was granted, the appeal allowed, and the certificate quashed.
Key holding

Where a respondent maintains a dispute that an applicant was not dismissed, the Commission must determine that antecedent jurisdictional issue before exercising its powers under s 368 or issuing a s 368(3) certificate.

For those reasons, it was not open to the Commissioner to issue a certificate under s 368(3) without first determining whether Ms Baker had been dismissed.

Ms Anne-Marie Snelling and Ms Meta Jackman v Fr Stephen Hackett, Lokimi Vunivesilevu and Others [2026] FWC 808 (13 March 2026)

13 Mar 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 808Employment & workplace
The applicants sought to amend their s.365 dismissal applications to substitute seven named individuals, including Fr Stephen Hackett, for a corporate entity. The respondents objected that Fr Hackett was outside the leave to amend and that there was no employment relationship with the individual respondents. The Commission rejected both objections and listed the matters for conference.
Key holding

A valid application under s.365 requires only that the applicant has been dismissed and alleges the dismissal contravened Part 3-1; it does not require the named respondent to be the employer or the dismissal to have been effected by that respondent.

Civmec stands for the proposition that a valid application under s.365 is conditioned only by a requirement that a person has been dismissed and that the dismissal is alleged by the person to be in contravention of Part 3-1. If those conditions are met, then there is a valid application under s.365 and the Commission is able to 'deal with dispute' under s.368.

Les Kalmar v Meliian Pty Ltd [2026] FWC 809 (12 March 2026)

12 Mar 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 809Employment & workplace
Les Kalmar lodged a s 365 general protections dismissal dispute application alleging Meliian Pty Ltd contravened his workplace rights when it terminated his employment on 19 November 2025. Meliian objected that Kalmar was not an employee and therefore not dismissed within s 386 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth). The Commission rejected that objection, found Kalmar was an employee and dismissed, and listed the matter for conciliation under s 368.
Key holding

Where dismissal is disputed, the Commission must determine whether the person was dismissed before exercising its powers under s 368.

Where there is a dispute about whether a person was dismissed, the Commission must determine that point before exercising its powers under s 368 of the Act.

Sandra Kuzman v The Trustee for Nair Family Trust, Robin Goh [2026] FWC 812 (12 March 2026)

12 Mar 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 812Employment & workplace
The Fair Work Commission dismissed jurisdictional objections to a general protections dismissal application brought by an overseas-based administrative worker. It found the applicant had been dismissed by an Australian corporate employer, Goh RGYH Pty Ltd, and was an Australian-based employee despite performing work in Egypt. The matter will proceed to a s 368 conference.
Key holding

For s 35(3) of the Fair Work Act, an employee is “engaged” at the place the employment contract is made; an electronic acceptance received at the Australian employer’s registered place does not activate the overseas-engagement exclusion.

The Applicant was therefore 'engaged' by Goh RGYH Pty Ltd at the time when the employment contract was made. The 'place' where the employment contract was made, and where the Applicant was thus engaged, was at the Respondent's registered place of business in Western Australia.

Brent Houston v AWX Pty Ltd [2026] FWC 702 (3 March 2026)

3 Mar 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 702Employment & workplace
Brent Houston brought a s 365 general protections application against labour-hire employer AWX Pty Ltd after his placement ended and AWX issued a separation certificate citing “shortage of work”. AWX objected that the Commission lacked jurisdiction because Mr Houston had not been dismissed. Deputy President Colman found the employment ended on the employer’s initiative, dismissed the objection, and issued a s 368 certificate while indicating no reasonable prospect of success under s 369 or in court.
Key holding

Where an employer issues a separation certificate citing shortage of work and does not confirm or record that a casual employee is resigning, the employment is treated as terminated on the employer’s initiative and the employee is dismissed under s 386(1)(a).

But if AWX believes the casual is resigning, it should confirm this and also state in the certificate that the employee is ceasing work voluntarily. In my view Mr Houston's employment was terminated on the employer's initiative, and he was therefore dismissed within the meaning of s 386(1)(a) of the Act.

February 2026 (2)

Balaji Bavuri v ProQuest Pty Ltd, Sarah Martin [2026] FWC 639 (26 February 2026)

26 Feb 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 639Employment & workplace
Balaji Bavuri, a casual store worker employed by labour-hire company ProQuest and placed with client TTi, lodged a s 365 general protections application alleging dismissal on 28 October 2025. ProQuest objected that he was not dismissed within the meaning of s 386 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth). The Fair Work Commission found he was dismissed and dismissed the jurisdictional objection; the matter will proceed to a s 368 conference.
Key holding

A casual labour-hire employee is dismissed at the employer’s initiative under s 386(1)(a) where the employer’s conduct—including removal from a client roster and communications that the employee has been terminated—brings the employment relationship to an end without the employee’s agreement.

It follows from the foregoing that the employment of the Applicant ended at the initiative of the Respondent within the meaning of s 386(1)(a) of the Act.

Alana Woolston v Department Of Education Qld [2026] FWC 611 (25 February 2026)

25 Feb 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 611Employment & workplace
Ms Woolston brought a general protections application under s 365 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth), alleging dismissal by Hudson Global Resources and knowing involvement by the State of Queensland. The Commission had to decide whether she had been dismissed within s 386 before exercising s 368 powers. It held that the end of her labour-hire assignment with the State did not end her casual employment with Hudson, so the application was dismissed.
Key holding

The termination by a labour-hire employer of a single client assignment does not, without more, terminate the employee’s employment with the labour-hire employer for the purposes of s 386.

In circumstances where a labour hire company, such as Hudson, terminates one assignment on which an employee is working, the termination of the assignment does not, without more, terminate the employee's employment with the labour hire company.
