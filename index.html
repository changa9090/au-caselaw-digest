Darlington-Thomas v Elgas Limited

21 Oct 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 1566Migration: court challengeMigration
The Federal Court granted Ms Darlington-Thomas leave under s 46PO(3A)(a) of the Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986 (Cth) to proceed against her labour hire employer, Zoom, for alleged disability discrimination. The AHRC had terminated her complaint against Zoom, but the Court held the claim was reasonably arguable and should be resolved with the overlapping claim against Elgas.
Key holding

On an application for leave under s 46PO(3A)(a) of the AHRC Act, the Court asks only whether the proposed discrimination claim is reasonably arguable and not fanciful, rather than determining the merits.

I am satisfied that the text, context and purpose of the leave requirement in s 46PO(3A) suggests that it is appropriate for the Court to consider in determining whether to grant leave whether the claims made by an applicant are reasonably arguable, and are - at the least - not fanciful. This is consistent with the language used in s 46P(1A).

Watson v Director of Public Prosecutions (NSW)

21 Oct 2026New South Wales Court of Appeal[2026] NSWCA 222Migration: court challengeAdministrative law
Before: Stern JA at [1]Free JA at [2]Price AJA at [98] Decision: (1) The amended summons
Mr Watson sought judicial review in the NSW Court of Appeal of Beckett DCJ’s refusal under s 5B of the Criminal Appeal Act 1912 (NSW) to submit questions of law to the Court of Criminal Appeal after his conviction appeal was dismissed. The Court of Appeal dismissed the amended summons, holding that no jurisdictional error was shown in her Honour’s treatment of the proposed questions. Costs were awarded to the DPP.
Key holding

On judicial review of a s 5B decision, an error by the District Court in characterising a proposed question as law, fact or mixed fact and law is ordinarily an error of law within jurisdiction and therefore not a jurisdictional error.

Baraboutis and CEO, National Disability Insurance Agency (NDIS)

20 Oct 2026Administrative Review Tribunal[2026] ARTA 2417Tribunal reviewMigration
Ms Baraboutis sought review of a decision refusing her access to the NDIS. The Tribunal accepted she had physical and neurological impairments and that some were permanent, but was not satisfied they caused substantially reduced functional capacity under s 24(1)(c) of the NDIS Act. It affirmed the refusal.
Key holding

In assessing whether impairments result in substantially reduced functional capacity under s 24(1)(c), the Tribunal must consider each specified activity as a whole rather than by reference to isolated tasks.

Further, the requirement to consider an activity as a whole, rather than by reference to tasks considered in isolation (as made plain in the amendment to s 24(1)(c)) reflects the reasoning of the Full Court of the Federal Court in National Disability Insurance Agency v Foster (2023) 295 FCR 521; [2023] FCAFC 11 ('Foster').

BNE24 v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship

20 Oct 2026Federal Court of Australia[2026] FCA 1561Migration: court challengeMigration
This was an appeal from the Federal Circuit and Family Court (Division 2) dismissing review of a Tribunal decision refusing a protection visa. The unrepresented appellant argued the Tribunal’s adverse credibility findings were illogical or unreasonable. The Federal Court dismissed the appeal, holding the primary judge correctly found no jurisdictional error.
Key holding

A Tribunal’s adverse credibility finding does not involve jurisdictional error merely because a reviewing court would have reached a different factual finding; the reasoning must be legally unreasonable.

Therefore, jurisdictional error in the reasoning by the Tribunal as to the credibility of the account given by an applicant is not established by seeking to persuade a court on review that the Tribunal's reasoning includes an error in the sense that the Court would not make the same finding.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
