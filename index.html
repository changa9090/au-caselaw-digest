Sarah Mcinnes v Le Luxe Design & Construct Pty Ltd

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 4064Employment & workplace
Ms McInnes filed an unfair dismissal application on 20 August 2026, 87 days after her employment was said to have ended on 25 May 2026, outside the 21-day statutory period. The Fair Work Commission was not satisfied there were exceptional circumstances and dismissed the extension application.
Key holding

Under s 394(3) of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth), the Commission must be satisfied that exceptional circumstances exist before extending time for an unfair dismissal application, regardless of the length of delay.

Regardless of the length of the delay, where an application is lodged outside the statutory time limit, the Commission must be satisfied that there are exceptional circumstances supporting an extension of time.

Michiaki Miyazaki v Sydney Omakase Pty Ltd

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 3383Employment & workplace
Miyazaki v Sydney Omakase Pty Ltd [2026] FWC 3383 concerned an unfair dismissal claim by a Head Chef dismissed for alleged drinking at work. The Fair Work Commission found the employer’s summary dismissal was not consistent with the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code and that the dismissal was harsh, unjust and unreasonable. Compensation was awarded.
Key holding

A dismissal is not consistent with the Summary Dismissal limb of the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code where the employer’s actual reason is a performance concern rather than a belief, held on reasonable grounds at dismissal, that the employee’s conduct was sufficiently serious to justify immediate dismissal.

I do not accept that Mr Best, on behalf of Sydney Omakase, held a belief that Mr Miyazaki's conduct was sufficiently serious to justify immediate dismissal. This is because I find that the reason for dismissal, based on Mr Best's witness statement, was that Mr Miyazaki was unable to confirm that he had the passion to continue as Head Chef.

Kacy Brazier v OS MCAP Pty Ltd

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 4043Employment & workplace
This Fair Work Commission decision concerns an order for lost pay following an earlier finding that Kacy Brazier was unfairly dismissed and an order for his reinstatement. The Commission awarded reduced lost pay under s 391 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) because Brazier made no real attempt to find paid work for most of his unemployment and performed unpaid work for his then partner. The total compensation ordered was $98,965.03 gross less tax plus $11,875.80 superannuation.
Key holding

An order to restore lost pay is discretionary and a deliberate failure to mitigate loss may reduce or defeat any award.

It cannot be said that he was unsophisticated or unaware that a deliberate failure to mitigate his loss would affect any award of lost pay at the Commission's discretion.

Application by Transport Workers' Union of Australia (179V)

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 4051Employment & workplace
Deputy President Hampton determined a s.437 protected action ballot order application by the TWU for Cleanaway employees covered by three regional Victorian waste enterprise agreements. The employer objected to scope and ballot questions 5, 9 and 10, including safety-related wording; the Commission rejected the continuing objections and granted the application, with leave to amend some questions. The ballot will be run by Fair Vote Services Pty Ltd and close on 5 November 2026.
Key holding

Under s.443(1) and (3)(d), where a valid s.437 application proposes clear ballot questions, the Commission must make a PABO and has no general discretion to exclude valid questions or amend them merely due to employer objections to wording.

The inference to be drawn from the mandatory nature of s 443(1) is that the order required to be made is one which gives effect to an application validly made under s 437. Thus, in respect of s 443(3)(d), we do not consider that the Commission has a general discretion to determine the questions which will be included in the order, or to simply exclude valid questions, independent of what has been applied for.

Application by Southern Cross Care

The Fair Work Commission dismissed the application after weighing the s 318(3) considerations. Employee disadvantage and the public interest weighed against the transfer.
Key holding

The Commission may decline orders under s 318(1)(a) where the mandatory s 318(3) considerations, especially employee disadvantage and the public interest, weigh against the transfer.

On a whole, taking into account the mandatory considerations under s 318(3), I do not consider it appropriate to exercise my discretion to make the orders sought. Particularly, I consider the disadvantage to the employees and public interest are matters which should be given more weight in this application, and which weigh against the orders sought.

Roberto Biguzzi v Eco Alliance Pty. Ltd.

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 3872Employment & workplace
The Fair Work Commission dealt with an unfair dismissal application by a former Senior Sales Representative whose role was made redundant after the employer closed its internal sales function. The employer asserted genuine redundancy under s 389, but the Commission found the job was no longer required due operational requirements while the written consultation did not satisfy cl 27.2 of the Miscellaneous Award 2020. The provided extract does not include final orders on the remaining s 387 merits.
Key holding

For a genuine redundancy defence under s 389(1)(b), written consultation must explain the nature of the major change itself, not merely its effects or causes.

It would in my view be insufficient to meet the written notice requirements in clause 27.2 to deal only with the effects of the changes without explaining what the changes actually are. This is consistent with "nature" and "effect" being separately itemised in paragraphs (a) and (b) of clause 27.2.

Peter Andrew Staas v Assta Labels Pty Ltd

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 4050Employment & workplace
The Fair Work Commission determined an unfair dismissal application by Peter Staas against his family company employer, Assta Labels Pty Ltd, under s.394 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth). Mr Staas was dismissed after repeatedly refusing to attend an independent medical examination, and the Commission found there was a valid reason and procedural fairness, dismissing the application.
Key holding

A direction to attend an independent medical examination can be lawful and reasonable—and refusal can supply a valid reason for dismissal—where the employee’s health is causally linked to ongoing performance concerns and the employer seeks to assess capacity and possible adjustments.

I am satisfied that the direction to attend an independent medical examination was lawful and reasonable each time a direction was given to Peter in the last four months of his employment. Peter 's refusal to follow this lawful and reasonable direction was a valid reason to dismiss him from his employment with Assta Labels.

Mr Muhammad Ahsan Malik v On The Run Pty Ltd

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 4054Employment & workplace
Mr Malik filed an unfair dismissal application 23 days after the 21-day statutory period expired. The Commission refused an extension because he had no satisfactory explanation for the delay, knew of the dismissal immediately, and the remaining factors were neutral or only marginally supportive. The application was dismissed.
Key holding

In weighing the s 394(3) factors, a marginal favour from the applicant’s efforts to dispute dismissal is outweighed by the lack of a satisfactory reason for delay and immediate awareness of dismissal, so no exceptional circumstances are established.

Balancing the factor that weighs marginally in favour of a finding of exceptional circumstances, being the Applicant's efforts to dispute his dismissal, against the factors weighing more heavily against such a finding, being the lack of a satisfactory reason for delay and the Applicant's awareness of the dismissal on the day it was conveyed by the Respondent, I find that there are no exceptional circumstances in this matter.

Key holding

A dismissal is a genuine redundancy where the employer establishes the s.389(1) matters and that reasonable redeployment was not available in all the circumstances under s.389(2).

Delaney King v Advanced Mobile IT Pty Limited

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 4047Employment & workplace
Ms King applied under s 394 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) for an unfair dismissal remedy after AMIT made her part-time finance/administrative role redundant in March 2026. The Commission had to decide whether the dismissal was a genuine redundancy under s 389, so that it was not unfair under s 385.
Key holding

A dismissal is a genuine redundancy only if all three limbs of s 389 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) are established: operational change removing the need for the job, consultation compliance, and no reasonable redeployment.

All three limbs of s 389 must be established for a dismissal to be a genuine redundancy.

Mr David Peter Grant v Metric PCM

19 Oct 2026Fair Work Commission[2026] FWC 3937Employment & workplace
The Fair Work Commission considered whether David Grant’s dismissal by Metric PCM Pty Ltd was a genuine redundancy after the EDL Energy contract ended. It found all three elements of genuine redundancy under s 389 of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) were satisfied, notwithstanding informal consultation and temporary alternative duties. Metric PCM’s jurisdictional objection was upheld and Mr Grant’s unfair dismissal application was dismissed.
Key holding

A dismissal meeting the s 389 definition of genuine redundancy cannot be an unfair dismissal because the person cannot satisfy the definition in s 385.

Given my three findings above, Mr Grant's dismissal was a case of "genuine redundancy" as defined in s.389 of the FW Act. That means Mr Grant cannot satisfy the definition of an unfair dismissal in s.385 of the FW Act.

Barresi v Cross Yarra Partnerships

19 Oct 2026Supreme Court of Victoria[2026] VSC 666Migration: court challengeMigration
Before: Clements J
The plaintiff sought judicial review of a second Wrongs Act Medical Panel determination that his whole person impairment did not meet the threshold, after an earlier panel had found that it did. The Court followed Citywide Service Solutions Pty Ltd v Rosata in permitting the later panel to determine the same medical question, and rejected the challenge based on s 28LZG(4). The catchwords record “Appeal dismissed”.
Key holding

An asserted misunderstanding by a Medical Panel of s 28LZG(4) will not constitute jurisdictional error unless the misunderstanding was material to the determination, in the sense that the decision could realistically have been different.

Consequently, in my opinion, the plaintiff has not established that the asserted misunderstanding by the second Panel of s 28LZG(4) was material to the decision that the second Panel made and therefore jurisdictional error has not been established.

Legal words, explained

Medium neutral citation (e.g. [2026] NSWSC 1234)
The standard reference number Australian courts give each decision: year, court code, and sequence number. It identifies the decision even before it appears in a law report.
Court / tribunal
Courts decide disputes between parties and can hear appeals. Tribunals (like the ART) re-examine government decisions on their merits, in a quicker, less formal process.
Judicial review
A court checking whether a government or tribunal decision was legally made. The court looks at legal errors, not whether it agrees with the outcome.
Merits review
A tribunal (usually the Administrative Review Tribunal) re-making a government decision from scratch, considering all the facts again.
Visa cancellation (s 501)
Under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958, a visa can be refused or cancelled on 'character' grounds, often because of a criminal record. Many tribunal cases on this site are appeals against such cancellations.
Set aside / affirmed
When a tribunal or appeal court 'sets aside' a decision, it is cancelled and usually replaced. 'Affirmed' means the original decision stands.
Applicant / respondent
The applicant is the person who brought the case; the respondent is the party responding to it (in tribunal matters, often a government minister or agency).
